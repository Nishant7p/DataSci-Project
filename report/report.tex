\section{Basic questions and problem statement}

\field{Split before exploring:}{we shuffled the 51{,}348 hosts with seed 558 and put 30\% of them,
with all their listings, in an exploration split (15{,}404 hosts, 27{,}586 listings); the other
35{,}944 hosts (64{,}189 listings) form the confirmation split. Splitting by host keeps one
operator's listings on one side. Figures 1--4 use the exploration split only; Section 5 uses the
confirmation split only. Every listing's side is saved in
\code{outputs/tables/split\_assignment.csv.gz}.}

\minihead{Basic questions (quick answers from the exploration split)}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item Which boroughs are the most expensive? (Westminster, City of London, Kensington and
  Chelsea: median entire home \pounds295--\pounds304 a night)
  \item Do entire homes cost more than private rooms? (median \pounds224 against \pounds80)
  \item Do Superhosts get higher ratings? (4.86 against 4.68 on average)
  \item Do listings with more reviews rate higher? (barely: Spearman $\rho=0.01$)
\end{enumerate}

\par\smallskip\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\textbf{Our problem statements}\par\smallskip
\noindent\fbox{\begin{minipage}{\dimexpr\linewidth-2\fboxsep-2\fboxrule\relax}
\textbf{P1 (primary).} Among comparable entire homes (same borough and number of bedrooms), do
listings run by hosts with 10 or more London listings get different guest ratings from
listings run by single-listing hosts?

\smallskip
\textbf{P2.} Among established entire homes, do listings whose descriptions pile on marketing
superlatives get a different accuracy score (the guests' rating of whether the place matched
its page) from plainly described ones?
\end{minipage}}
\end{minipage}


\field{How they are novel:}{the basic questions read one column of a listing against another.
Ours depend on who runs the listing, a portfolio size that no row records directly (we recount
it per host after removing duplicates), compared like for like within 99 borough-by-bedroom
strata; and on a feature built from the description text, matched to the one score that
measures whether a listing kept its word.}

\field{Causal flavour:}{P1 asks whether big operators ``drag ratings down'', which sounds causal.
Our design only holds borough and size fixed; operators also differ in property type, pricing
and guests. A causal claim would need the same property observed before and after a change of
operator (quarterly snapshots as a panel) or random assignment, which a single snapshot cannot
give.}

\section{Statistical inferences}

\field{Rows tested:}{the 17{,}929 established entire homes (10+ reviews) of the confirmation split.
P1 compares the 7{,}197 listings of single-listing hosts ($S$) with the 5{,}288 listings of the
477 hosts with 10+ London listings ($L$); the 5{,}439 listings of hosts with 2--9 listings (a
middle group, not used) and 5 without a bedroom count are left out, leaving 12{,}485 listings
in 99 borough-by-bedroom-band strata (0--1, 2, 3+), 96 of which contain both groups. P2 keeps one
random listing per host (seed 558): 2{,}470 \emph{hyped} listings (2+ hype words) and 4{,}908
\emph{plain} ones (none). The four tests form one family: decisions use Holm-adjusted p-values
at a family-wise $\alpha=0.05$. All settings were saved in \code{docs/preregistration.json}
before any test ran.}

%------------------------------------------------------------ Test 1
\subsection*{Test 1 (P1): does a comparable large-host listing rate differently?}

\tpart{a}{Hypotheses, written before running the test}
{\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}K{3.4cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
$H_0$ & $\beta=0$ \\
$H_1$ & $\beta\neq0$ \\
Significance level & $\alpha=0.05$ (family-wise, Holm) \\
Side & two-sided \\
Test & t-test on the within-stratum gap with host-clustered standard errors \\
Smallest difference that matters & 0.10 star \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

$\beta$: gap in mean overall rating, $L$ minus $S$, within borough-by-bedroom strata.
Two-sided, because professional operators could plausibly rate higher (consistent service) as
well as lower. 0.10 star, because the middle half of established ratings spans only 4.62 to
4.91, and 0.10 star moves a median listing (4.79) across Airbnb's 4.8 Superhost bar.

\tpart{b}{Other tests we considered}
Two independent groups, a continuous response and an unknown $\sigma$ call for a two-sample
t-test, with two changes the data forces. Borough confounds host type (Figure 3), so we
compare within strata (one intercept per stratum). And a large host's ratings share one
operator, so they are not independent: we use host-clustered (CR1) standard errors and $t$ with
$G-1$ degrees of freedom, $G$ being the number of hosts.

{\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.3cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Also considered} & \textbf{Why we did not use it} \\
\midrule
Welch t-test & ignores strata and clustering; gives $-0.154$ ($t=-39.1$, 9{,}512 d.f.) with a
standard error a third of the clustered one (0.0039 against 0.0118) \\
Pooled t-test & assumes equal spreads, which Brown--Forsythe rejects (SD 0.239 against 0.182,
$F=416.9$, $p<0.001$) \\
Mann--Whitney U & tests a shift in ranks, not stars, and ties at 5.0 are common; agrees in
direction: $P(L>S)=0.29$, $p<0.001$ \\
Stratified permutation & assumes no distribution and gives the same answer ($-0.149$,
$p=0.0001$, the smallest possible with 10{,}000 permutations), but treats listings in a stratum
as exchangeable, ignoring the host clusters \\
z-test, paired t-test & $\sigma$ unknown; nothing to pair \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

\tpart{c}{Result}
{\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}K{3.4cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
Estimate & $-0.145$ star (raw means 4.66 for $L$, 4.81 for $S$) \\
95\% confidence interval & $-0.168$ to $-0.122$ \\
Test statistic (d.f.) & $t=-12.34$ (7{,}673) \\
p-value & $1.1\times10^{-34}$ (Holm: $2.3\times10^{-34}$) \\
Decision & reject $H_0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

Comparable large-host homes rate 0.145 star lower. The whole interval lies beyond $-0.10$ star,
so the gap matters by our standard. It also survives a conservative 476 degrees of freedom (one
per large host, $p\approx10^{-30}$).

%------------------------------------------------------------ Test 2
\subsection*{Test 2 (P1): do large-host listings miss the Superhost bar more often?}

\tpart{a}{Hypotheses, written before running the test}
{\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}K{3.4cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
$H_0$ & $\theta=1$ \\
$H_1$ & $\theta\neq1$ \\
Significance level & $\alpha=0.05$ (family-wise, Holm) \\
Side & two-sided \\
Test & Cochran--Mantel--Haenszel (CMH) \\
Smallest difference that matters & odds ratio outside 0.80--1.25 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

$\theta$: common odds ratio, across strata, of a rating below 4.8 for $L$ against $S$. 4.8 is
the rating Airbnb requires for Superhost status; at the exploration single-host rate of 33.8\%
below it, an odds ratio of 1.25 means one more listing in twenty falls short.

\tpart{b}{Other tests we considered}
A 0/1 outcome compared between two groups across 96 strata calls for the CMH test, which pools
the strata's $2\times2$ tables without fitting a model.

{\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.3cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Also considered} & \textbf{Why we did not use it} \\
\midrule
Pooled $\chi^2$, two-proportion z-test & ignore the strata; give $\chi^2=1{,}162$ ($z=34.1$,
$z^2=\chi^2$) and an odds ratio of 3.59, inflated by the borough mix \\
Fisher's exact test & meant for small counts; with 12{,}443 listings the $\chi^2$ approximation
is accurate \\
Logistic regression with stratum terms & asks the same question through a model; CMH needs
none \\
Breslow--Day (assumption check) & rejects a common odds ratio ($\chi^2=223.4$, 95 d.f.), so the
estimate is an average over strata; with one listing per host it does not reject ($p=0.20$) and
the odds ratio is 3.06 (2.50 to 3.73) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

\tpart{c}{Result}
{\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}K{3.4cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
Estimate & odds ratio 3.22 (65.5\% of $L$ and 34.6\% of $S$ below 4.8) \\
95\% confidence interval & 2.97 to 3.49 \\
Test statistic (d.f.) & $\chi^2_{\mathrm{MH}}=838.2$ (1) \\
p-value & $2.7\times10^{-184}$ (Holm: $1.1\times10^{-183}$) \\
Decision & reject $H_0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

Within the same borough and bedroom band, a large-host listing has 3.2 times the odds of falling
below the Superhost bar. The whole interval lies above 1.25, so the difference matters.

%------------------------------------------------------------ Test 3
\subsection*{Test 3 (P1): with every host counted once, do large hosts rate lower?}

\tpart{a}{Hypotheses, written before running the test}
{\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}K{3.4cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
$H_0$ & $\mu_L-\mu_S=0$ \\
$H_1$ & $\mu_L-\mu_S\neq0$ \\
Significance level & $\alpha=0.05$ (family-wise, Holm) \\
Side & two-sided \\
Test & Welch t-test \\
Smallest difference that matters & 0.10 star \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

$\mu_L$, $\mu_S$: mean rating of a host's established entire homes, averaged per host (477
large hosts, 7{,}197 single hosts). Averaging removes the dependence within a host by design
rather than by a model.

\tpart{b}{Other tests we considered}
Two independent groups of hosts, very unequal in size and spread: Welch's t-test.

{\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.3cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Also considered} & \textbf{Why we did not use it} \\
\midrule
Pooled t-test & Brown--Forsythe rejects equal variances ($F=9.58$, $p=0.002$); run anyway, it
gives $t=-18.96$ and the same decision \\
Mann--Whitney U & tests ranks, not stars; agrees: $P(L>S)=0.24$, $p<0.001$ \\
Permutation test & also valid; $p=0.0002$, the smallest two-sided value 10{,}000 resamples can
give \\
Stratified tests & a host's listings span several boroughs, so host means cannot be stratified;
Tests 1 and 2 cover that \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

\tpart{c}{Result}
{\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}K{3.4cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
Estimate & $-0.164$ star (host means 4.65 for $L$, 4.81 for $S$) \\
95\% confidence interval & $-0.182$ to $-0.146$ \\
Test statistic (d.f.) & $t=-17.85$ (532.4) \\
p-value & $3.4\times10^{-56}$ (Holm: $1.0\times10^{-55}$) \\
Decision & reject $H_0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

The typical large operator rates 0.16 star below the typical single host. The interval lies
beyond $-0.10$ star, so this gap matters too.

%------------------------------------------------------------ Test 4
\needspace{24\baselineskip}  % keep the heading with its hypotheses table
\subsection*{Test 4 (P2): do hyped descriptions change the accuracy score?}

\tpart{a}{Hypotheses, written before running the test}
{\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}K{3.4cm} Y@{}}
\toprule
$H_0$ & $\delta=0$ \\
$H_1$ & $\delta\neq0$ \\
Significance level & $\alpha=0.05$ (family-wise, Holm) \\
Side & two-sided \\
Test & stratified permutation test (10{,}000 permutations) \\
Smallest difference that matters & 0.10 star \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}}

$\delta$: gap in mean accuracy score, hyped minus plain, averaged over borough-by-host-class
strata. Hyped means 2 or more words from a fixed list of 34 superlatives (``perfect'',
``stunning'', ``luxury'', \dots): the largest count still covering 15\% of exploration listings
(23.8\% have 2+). Two-sided, because the exploration split showed no direction (Figure 4).
0.10 star, because Airbnb shows category scores such as accuracy to one decimal.

\tpart{b}{Other tests we considered}

one listing per host stops big operators' copied descriptions from counting many times.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Association, not cause.} Large operators differ from single hosts in more than
  size (property type, pricing, guests, house rules); our strata hold only borough and bedrooms
  fixed.
  \item \textbf{Established entire homes, one snapshot.} The tests cover entire homes with 10+
  reviews on 17 September 2026, not private rooms, new listings or listings that had already
  left Airbnb.
  \item \textbf{Compressed ratings.} Half of established ratings lie between 4.62 and 4.91, and
  each average rests on a different number of reviews; our tests do not weight by that
  precision.
  \item \textbf{Dependence handled only in part.} Test 1 clusters by host, but only 477 clusters
  carry the treatment, so its 7{,}673 d.f. may be generous (with 476 d.f. the result stands).
  Test 2 treats listings as independent; with one listing per host its odds ratio is 3.06.
  \item \textbf{A pooled odds ratio over unequal strata.} Breslow--Day rejects a common odds
  ratio, so 3.22 averages different stratum effects.
  \item \textbf{Missing prices are not random.} 35\% of listings have no price because they cannot
  be booked; conclusions about price would not generalise, which is why no test uses it.
  \item \textbf{A crude hype measure.} A fixed English word list misses context (``perfect for
  families'') and other languages, and host size counts London listings only (Airbnb-wide
  portfolios reach 5{,}777).
\end{itemize}
